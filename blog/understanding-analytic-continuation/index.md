# Understanding Analytic Continuation

*Published: September 2, 2026*
*Category: Analytic Continuation*

[Analytic continuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_continuation) extends a function beyond its original domain in a way that preserves its analytic structure. This post presents two elementary examples: a punctured domain, and a restricted domain.

## Contents

1. [A punctured point](#punctured)
2. [Continuity versus analyticity](#regularity)
3. [A restricted domain](#restricted)

<a id="punctured"></a>
## 1. A punctured point

Consider

$$f(x) = \frac{x^{2}}{x}.$$

The domain of $f$ is $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$, so $f(0)$ is undefined.

The function $g(x) = x$ agrees with $f$ on the domain of $f$ and is continuous, differentiable, and [analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_function) on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

The [Taylor series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_series) of $f$ centered at any $x_{0} \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ is

$$f(x) \;=\; x_{0} \;+\; 1 \cdot (x - x_{0}) \;+\; 0 \cdot \frac{(x - x_{0})^{2}}{2!} \;+\; \cdots$$

The [radius of convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius_of_convergence) is $R = \infty$.

<details class="bulb">
<summary>Compute R via the Ratio Test or Cauchy–Hadamard</summary>

The [Ratio Test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratio_test):

$$\frac{1}{R} \;=\; \lim_{k \to \infty} \left|\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_{k}}\right|.$$

The [Cauchy–Hadamard formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy%E2%80%93Hadamard_theorem):

$$\frac{1}{R} \;=\; \limsup_{k \to \infty} \sqrt[k]{|a_{k}|}.$$

For $g(x) = x$ centered at $x_{0} \neq 0$, only $a_{0} = x_{0}$ and $a_{1} = 1$ are nonzero, so $1/R = 0$ and $R = \infty$.

</details>

$R = \infty$ means the Taylor expansion of $g$ centered at any $x_{0} \neq 0$ recovers $g$ everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$, including at $x = 0$ where $f$ was undefined. The value $g(0) = 0$ is the analytic continuation of $f$ to $x = 0$.

<div class="intuition"><strong>Intuition.</strong> Analytic continuation to a punctured point is to a Taylor expansion what limit-based continuity is to a hole in a graph: a way to recover the value at a point where the original expression fails to be defined.</div>

## 2. Continuity versus analyticity

Four levels of regularity, from weakest to strongest:

![Table showing continuity, differentiability, smoothness, and analyticity, each with its criterion](assets/blogs/understanding-analytic-continuation/concept-table.svg)

*Generated by [`concept_table.py`](https://github.com/TensorTheorist/TensorTheorist.github.io/blob/main/scripts/blog/understanding-analytic-continuation/concept_table.py).*

- [Continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function) requires only that the two-sided limit exists and equals the function value.
- [Differentiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable_function) requires the derivative limit to exist from both sides and agree.
- [Smoothness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoothness) requires the same for every derivative.
- [Analyticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_function) requires the Taylor series to converge to $f$ on an open neighborhood of $x_{0}$.

Analyticity is strictly stronger than smoothness. A standard counter-example is the [smooth non-analytic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-analytic_smooth_function) $\varphi(x) = e^{-1/x^{2}}$ (with $\varphi(0) = 0$), whose Taylor series at $x = 0$ is identically zero yet $\varphi$ is not identically zero.

Returning to $f(x) = x^{2}/x$: filling in the value $f(0) = 0$ by continuity would only recover the limit. The Taylor expansion of the analytic continuation $g$ recovers the same value, but from data at any nearby point.

<a id="restricted"></a>
## 3. A restricted domain

Consider the real [geometric series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_series)

$$f(x) \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} x^{k}.$$

The series converges only for $|x| < 1$. On that interval it sums to

$$f(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 - x}, \qquad |x| < 1.$$

Now consider

$$g(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 - x}, \qquad x \neq 1.$$

$g$ agrees with $f$ on $(-1, 1)$ and is defined everywhere else on $\mathbb{R}$ except at $x = 1$, where it has a [simple pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_(complex_analysis)). $g$ is the analytic continuation of $f$ to $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$.

![Plot of 1/(1 - x) with the convergence interval highlighted and g(2) = -1 marked](assets/blogs/understanding-analytic-continuation/geometric-plot.svg)

*Generated by [`geometric_plot.py`](https://github.com/TensorTheorist/TensorTheorist.github.io/blob/main/scripts/blog/understanding-analytic-continuation/geometric_plot.py).*

Evaluating $g$ at $x = 2$ gives $g(2) = -1$. The literal sum $1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots$ diverges. But $f$ is a restriction of $g$ to $(-1, 1)$, and $g$ takes the value $-1$ at $x = 2$, where the original series does not converge.

<div class="intuition"><strong>Intuition.</strong> The value of an analytic continuation outside the original domain is not the value of any convergent sum. It is the value of the underlying analytic function that agrees with the series on its domain of convergence.</div>

---

<div class="sneak-peek"><strong>Sneak peek.</strong> The Riemann zeta function is defined by a similar series that converges only for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1$. Its analytic continuation to $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{1\}$ is what underlies the <a href="blog-post.html?post=riemann-hypothesis-intro">Riemann Hypothesis</a>.</div>
