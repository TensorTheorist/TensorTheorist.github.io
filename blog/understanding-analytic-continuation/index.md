# Understanding Analytic Continuation

*Published: September 2, 2026*
*Category: Analytic Continuation*

---

## (1) A simple case: punctured point

Consider

$$f(x) = \frac{x^{2}}{x}.$$

This is not the same as $g(x) = x$ — they have different domains. We will show that $g(x) = x$ is the [analytic continuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_continuation) of $f(x) = x^{2}/x$.

$f(x) = x^{2}/x$ has domain $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$, so $f(0)$ is undefined. However the function $g(x) = x$ is exactly the same as $f(x)$ everywhere in the domain of $f(x)$. Additionally, $g(x)$ is continuous and differentiable everywhere ([analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_function)).

The [Taylor series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_series) expansion of $f(x)$ on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ is the same as that of $g(x) = x$. We have

$$f(x) = x_{0} + 1 \cdot (x - x_{0}) + 0 \cdot \frac{(x - x_{0})^{2}}{2!} + \cdots$$

Also the [radius of convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius_of_convergence) is $\infty$.

$$\Bigl[\, f(x) = f(x_{0}) + f'(x_{0}) \cdot (x - x_{0}) + f''(x_{0}) \cdot \frac{(x - x_{0})^{2}}{2!} + \cdots + \infty \,\Bigr]_{x = x_{0}}$$

Also calculate radius of convergence by either:

(1) [Ratio Test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratio_test)

$$\frac{1}{R} = \lim_{k \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_{k}} \right|$$

(2) [Cauchy–Hadamard formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy%E2%80%93Hadamard_theorem)

$$\frac{1}{R} = \limsup_{k \to \infty} \sqrt[k]{\,|a_{k}|\,}$$

$R = \infty$ means that the Taylor expansion holds around any $x_{0} \neq 0$. This is similar in idea to the function being [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function) at a point where it is undefined by having

$$\lim_{x \to x_{0}} f(x) = L,$$

where $L = \lim_{x \to x_{0}^{-}} f(x) = \lim_{x \to x_{0}^{+}} f(x)$. However this is only continuity and not [analyticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_function).

Now looking back at $f(x) = x^{2}/x$, this is not clearly defined at $x = 0$ but when analytically continued it can be said to have the value $0$ at $x = 0$.

## (2) A restricted domain

Consider now the real [geometric series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_series)

$$f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} x^{k}.$$

This clearly diverges when $|x| \geq 1$. We moreover have from the known formula for the geometric series,

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{1 - x}, \qquad |x| < 1.$$

Now consider

$$g(x) = \frac{1}{1 - x}, \qquad x \neq 1.$$

This is defined everywhere except a [pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_(complex_analysis)) at $x = 1$.

Putting $x = 2$, we see that $g(x) = -1$. Of course $1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots \to \infty$ does not equal $-1$ (it diverges). But if we consider $f(x)$ to be a restriction of $g(x)$ inside the limits of $(-1, 1)$, the underlying $g(x)$ is seen to equal $-1$ at the point $x = 2$ where $f(x)$ is not defined.
